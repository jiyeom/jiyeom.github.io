**Background**

I am a linguist, more specifically a formal semanticist. I studied GB syntax in Korea before I went to the University of Texas at Austin, where I studied formal semantics.

**Specific indefinites presuppose their quantificational force and descriptive contents.**

 My Ph.D. thesis was about specific indefinites. The main claim was that their exceptional scopes are determined by presupposition projection, assuming that specific indefinites presuppose the quantificational force and the descriptive content. I still find no other analysis that that can explain the exceptional scopes of indefinites. Another significance of the thesis is that it is the first serious proposal that presuppositions tend to be projected to convey the most information possible or the strongest meaning possible.

**Dynamic grammar**

While I taught the English Grammar at Hongik University, I was interested in the question of what kind of grammar English learners must aquire. Formal linguists just talk about the system itself, but language learners learn how to process sentences or discourses with the knowledge of grammar and pragmatic principles. A sequence of words often allows several structures in principle and each of the structures expects a different sequence of words to come. Native speakers find clues to choose one at each moment and switches easily from one structure to another. A grammar system itself is static, but the processing is dynamic. If a word is added to a sequence of words, possible continuations change. Learners must aquire the dynamic processing of a static grammar system. A grammar must be described in such a way. I wrote a book of the English Grammar as such an effort.

I started to do a long-term research project from 2010. From then on, I have proposed brand-new notions.

**Existence implicatures**

 The first one is a paper proposing a new kind of implicature, which I call existential implicatures. The basic idea is that an expression in a sentence makes a non-empty semantic contribution to the meaning of the sentence. If this idea applies to a disjunction sentence, we get from "You can eat an apple or a pear" the existence implicature that you can eat an apple and you can eat a pear. These implicatures arise because each disjunct must make a non-empty meaning contribution to the first quantifier that dominates the disjunction operator.

**Stage-denoting relative clauses**

The second one is a paper claiming that an internally headed relative clause in Korean can denote a stage. The first version of this paper is presented at a workshop at ZAS, Berlin. We often talk about stage-level predicates, but nothing in a sentence with a stage-level predicate denotes a stage. In Korean, however, an internally headed relative clause really can denote a stage. A stage of a person is not a person. For this reason the head noun "kes" ('thing' in English) can be used even when the relative clause must denote a human being.

**Non-contextual meanings**

1. Pseudoclefts

The third one is the notion of non-contextual meaning. I am still working on this notion. I was first interested in pseudoclefts in Korean. There are two constructions in which "kes" ('thing' in English) is used even when it must denote human beings. One is an internally headed relative clause and the other is a pseudocleft. From the analysis of an internally headed relative clause, I knew that "kes" can be used unless it should denote a person. Then I realized that "kes" in pseudoclefts denote an abstract meaning. I presented one paper at a workshop at Kyoto University. Later, I came up with the notion of non-contextual meaning to account for connectivity effects. A non-contextual meaning is an abstraction over assignments and possible worlds.

1. Kes-copula constructions

Non-contextual meanings arise due to focus on an expression. Therefore they can be involved in various constructions involving a focused phrase. One such construction is what I call a kes-copula construction. One characteristic of the construction is that it allows a "non-novel" indefinite, which is not allowed in ordinary sentences. The basic form of the construction is similar to "It is that ..." in English, but a non-novel indefinite is allowed in ordinary sentences in English. The construction is little known, and I will not explain how the notion of non-contextual meaning is involved in this construction.

1. Wh-questions with functional readings

Recently I wrote a third paper involving the notion of non-contextual meaning. A wh-question with the functional reading is often analyzed with a multiply indexed trace assuming that a wh-phrase denotes a function. It is very strange to say that a trace is multiply indexed and that "who" denotes a function. Moreover, a preposed wh-phrase with an anaphor must be reconstructed to explain the binding relations. On the other hand, a preposed wh-phrase must be interpreted in the surface position considering the intervention effect by Beck. Therefore a preposed wh-phrase must be interpreted in the surface position but it must have the effect of reconstruction. For this effect we need the notion of non-contextual meaning.

**Semantic binding**

For connectivity effects in pseudoclefts and the semantic effects of reconstruction of preposed wh-phrases, we need the notion of semantic binding, which is different from the one proposed by Heim and Kratzer's "Semantics in Generative Grammar". This the fourth notion. I find no syntactic solution of connectivity effects. Some syntactic analyses propose the process of copying and deleting a necessary structure to explain connectivity effects, but they cannot apply to Korean pseudoclefts. It follows that binding relations must be dealt with in semantics.

**Imperatives as world-changers**

The fifth notion is a world change. Uttering an imperative changes the truth value of a proposition. In possible world semantics, a set of possible worlds is given and a proposition with a necessity modality is evaluated with respect to a set of possible futures. An imperative changes the truth value of a proposition with a necessity modality. This means that the actual world in which the prejacent of the modal operator is true in all possible futures has changed to a possible world in which the prejacent of the modal operator is not true in all possible futures.

**Split information states**

The sixth notion is a split information state. In traditional dynamic semantics, an information state is a set of assignment-world pairs. Pairing an assignment with a possible world is problematic in dealing with double negation and cases where a dref introduced in a disjunct or a conditional is referred to by a pronoun as information increases. We need a dynamic semantics in which a dref is always kept regardless of where it is introduced. To deal with such cases, an assignment must not be paired with a possible world. I propose a split information state that consists of a world information state, which is a set of possible worlds,  and a discourse information state, which is a set of assignments. The idea of split information state was already proposed in my previous analysis of anaphora in attitude contexts, but it was incomplete. Recently the idea was elaborated so that it can apply to double negation, conditionals, and disjunction sentences. I took this topic again because a couple of analyses proposed to deal with double negation were not satisfactory.

# A data-driven personal website
# ======
# Like many other Jekyll-based GitHub Pages templates, Academic Pages makes you separate the website's content from its form. The content & metadata of your website are in structured Markdown files, while various other files constitute the theme, specifying how to transform that content & metadata into HTML pages. You keep these various Markdown (.md), YAML (.yml), HTML, and CSS files in a public GitHub repository. Each time you commit and push an update to the repository, the [GitHub pages](https://pages.github.com/) service creates static HTML pages based on these files, which are hosted on GitHub's servers free of charge.

# Many of the features of dynamic content management systems (like Wordpress) can be achieved in this fashion, using a fraction of the computational resources and with far less vulnerability to hacking and DDoSing. You can also modify the theme to your heart's content without touching the content of your site. If you get to a point where you've broken something in Jekyll/HTML/CSS beyond repair, your Markdown files describing your talks, publications, etc. are safe. You can rollback the changes or even delete the repository and start over - just be sure to save the Markdown files! You can also write scripts that process the structured data on the site, such as [this one](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/blob/master/talkmap.ipynb) that analyzes metadata in pages about talks to display [a map of every location you've given a talk](https://academicpages.github.io/talkmap.html).

# Getting started
# ======
# 1. Register a GitHub account if you don't have one and confirm your e-mail (required!)
# 1. Fork [this template](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io) by clicking the "Use this template" button in the top right. 
# 1. Go to the repository's settings (rightmost item in the tabs that start with "Code", should be below "Unwatch"). Rename the repository "[your GitHub username].github.io", which will also be your website's URL.
# 1. Set site-wide configuration and create content & metadata (see below -- also see [this set of diffs](https://archive.is/3TPas) showing what files were changed to set up [an example site](https://getorg-testacct.github.io) for a user with the username "getorg-testacct")
# 1. Upload any files (like PDFs, .zip files, etc.) to the files/ directory. They will appear at https://[your GitHub username].github.io/files/example.pdf.  
# 1. Check status by going to the repository settings, in the "GitHub pages" section

# Site-wide configuration
# ------
# The main configuration file for the site is in the base directory in [_config.yml](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/blob/master/_config.yml), which defines the content in the sidebars and other site-wide features. You will need to replace the default variables with ones about yourself and your site's github repository. The configuration file for the top menu is in [_data/navigation.yml](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/blob/master/_data/navigation.yml). For example, if you don't have a portfolio or blog posts, you can remove those items from that navigation.yml file to remove them from the header. 

# Create content & metadata
# ------
# For site content, there is one Markdown file for each type of content, which are stored in directories like _publications, _talks, _posts, _teaching, or _pages. For example, each talk is a Markdown file in the [_talks directory](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/tree/master/_talks). At the top of each Markdown file is structured data in YAML about the talk, which the theme will parse to do lots of cool stuff. The same structured data about a talk is used to generate the list of talks on the [Talks page](https://academicpages.github.io/talks), each [individual page](https://academicpages.github.io/talks/2012-03-01-talk-1) for specific talks, the talks section for the [CV page](https://academicpages.github.io/cv), and the [map of places you've given a talk](https://academicpages.github.io/talkmap.html) (if you run this [python file](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/blob/master/talkmap.py) or [Jupyter notebook](https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io/blob/master/talkmap.ipynb), which creates the HTML for the map based on the contents of the _talks directory).
