excerpt: 'This paper deals with three verbs that take a declarative clause. The verb <i>al</i> ‘know’ takes a <i>ko</i>-phrase, which is non-factive, and a <i>kes</i>-phrase, which describes a fact. The verb <i>mit</i> ‘believe’ also takes both, but <i>kes</i>-phrase taken by the verb denotes a proposition. The verb <i>sayngkakha</i> ‘think’ takes a <i>ko</i>-phrase, but not a <i>kes</i>-phrase. I claim that a <i>ko</i>-phrase is a predicate which reflects what is in the mind of the subject. A <i>kes</i>-phrase expresses something that exists independently of the epistemic process. It can be a fact or a proposition that is given. If a <i>kes</i>-phrase is selected by <i>al</i>, it denotes a fact and if a <i>kes</i>-phrase is selected by <i>mit</i>, it denotes a proposition. <i>sayngkakha</i> only expresses an epistemic state, and it does not take a <i>kes</i>-phrase. I also discuss an adnominal declarative clause as part of a <i>kes</i>-phrase.'

This paper deals with three propositional attitude verbs and compare their semantics: <i>al</i> 'know', <i>mit</i> 'believe' and <i>sayngkakha</i> 'think'. See also papers that deal with Mood Phrases.
